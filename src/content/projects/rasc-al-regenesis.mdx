<TabPanel id="overview">

REGENESIS (Regolith Engineering for Ground Expansion, Network Establishment, and Sustainable Infrastructure Systems) was CU Boulder's entry to Theme 4 of NASA's 2025–2026 RASC-AL competition, *Lunar Technology Demonstrations Leveraging Common Infrastructure*. The aim was to show that in-situ resources can supply the infrastructure for a future lunar economy, addressing Moon to Mars objectives LI-4, LI-5 and LI-7.

### The concept

Three payloads land in succession near the rim of Shackleton Crater at the lunar south pole, and each one sets up the next:

1. **Payload 1:** a rover, a solar furnace and a radio module. The furnace concentrates sunlight to sinter regolith into bricks at several heating and curing rates, and about forty bricks return to Earth for testing.
2. **Payload 2:** the rest of a solar-powered communications and charging tower, linked to NASA's planned LunaNet relay network. A launch pad is then built from interlocking regolith pavers.
3. **Payload 3:** lands on that pad and brings molds and an inflatable liner for a scaled regolith-brick shelter, instrumented to send structural and environmental data home.

### My role

I was team lead from August 2025 to April 2026 for a 16-member multidisciplinary team, working with aerospace faculty advisors. My own technical work was ANSYS simulation of how the launch pad holds up under lunar lander plume and landing loads. The concept, the report and its figures were team work.

### Working from the research literature

REGENESIS is a proposal, so its design choices rest on published work rather than on our own tests. What I took most from it was working with research documents: getting familiar with them, vetting how far each one could be trusted, and applying it to the specific situation we were in. The report shows this in a few places:

- **Motivating the pad.** *Dust transport and its effects due to landing spacecraft* set out the erosion problem the pad has to solve.
- **Choosing the material.** Rocket-motor tests on regolith–polymer composite pads (*In-situ lunar launch and landing pad construction with regolith-thermoset polymer composite materials*) and measured conductivities of sintered regolith (*Vacuum sintered lunar regolith simulant: pore-forming and thermal conductivity*) were the basis for using pure regolith pavers without a binder.
- **Weighting the sources.** Mass, power and cost margins were set by how well each number was backed: 0 % for a high-TRL datasheet, 20 % for high confidence, 40 % for medium and 70 % for low.
- **Matching standards.** The communications design follows the *LunaNet Interoperability Specification (LNIS) v5*.

### Launch pad

According to the cited study, lander exhaust can erode lunar soil up to 100 m deep and throw up dust. A paved pad keeps the plume off the surface. The report judges a pad on structural performance, flow penetration and heat propagation.

**Paver design.** The pad uses a single interlocking paver shape, so one mold makes the whole pad. The design drawings show two offset octagonal layers, 0.15 m on a side and 0.05 m thick per layer. The interlock is meant to stop exhaust from getting between pavers and to keep them from moving.

{/* TODO(Khalil): confirm the paver reading: is one paver the two offset octagons (red and green in the drawing), or are red and green two neighbouring pavers? */}

**Material.** The cited composite pads use a binder with a thermal conductivity of about 0.29 and reached TRL 5 in rocket-motor tests. Pure sintered regolith is about 0.26, so the team expected similar heat propagation without the Earth-supplied binder. The pad was thickened for the hotter exhaust of larger landers.

**Structural simulation.** The load case used the force output of a Blue Origin MK1 lander. The maximum pad deformation was 3.59 × 10⁻⁷ m under the lander legs and 1.63 × 10⁻⁷ m under the thruster load.

### Budgets

From the team's final report: a common mass of 882.3 kg, a common volume of 3.25 m³, a mean power draw of 1,400 W, and a total cost of $259M, of which $216.4M is launch. Margins on each subsystem put the total between $251.2M and $298.4M.

<TabPanel id="reflection">

The structural case is the only pad analysis in the report. Flow penetration and heat propagation were argued from the literature, and the report says a CFD simulation and a thermal simulation would be needed to settle them. Paver stability was assumed from the interlock.
