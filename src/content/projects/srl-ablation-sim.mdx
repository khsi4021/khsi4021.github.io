### The problem

A fast sounding rocket heats its nose, body and fin leading edges while still in the dense lower atmosphere. The CU Boulder Sounding Rocket Lab protects them with ablative material, typically a phenolic composite that chars and releases gas as it heats. The lab's thermal-protection team needs to know how hot each surface gets, and how thick the ablator must be to keep the bond line below its adhesive limit.

### What the simulator does

It reads a rocket geometry from a config file (eight nose shapes, blunting, body tubes, transitions, fins, a multilayer wall per component) and a trajectory spreadsheet. At each time step it:

1. finds the inviscid boundary-layer-edge state along the body and on the fins;
2. computes convective heating with published engineering methods (laminar, transitional or turbulent; hot wall; gas blowing; re-radiation);
3. solves transient finite-difference conduction through the wall at every station, with pyrolysis, gas flow and recession in a charring ablator.

Outputs include heat maps, timelines, a 2-D temperature view of the rocket, an animation and a report (examples from a synthetic design are on the Results tab), plus laminar / nominal / turbulent bounding runs and ablator sizing to a bond-line limit. It uses base MATLAB only, so every lab member can run it.

### Edge state and heating

The freestream is the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976. Normal-shock relations give the stagnation state, modified Newtonian theory the cap pressure, and a Taylor–Maccoll table the tangent-cone afterbody pressure. Heating uses a reference-temperature method with an enthalpy driving potential:

<Equation tex={String.raw`q_w = \rho^* u_e\,C_H^*\,(h_{aw} - h_w), \qquad \frac{T^*}{T_e} = 1 + 0.032\,M_e^2 + 0.58\left(\frac{T_w}{T_e} - 1\right)`} />

Turbulent flow uses c<sub>f</sub>\* = 0.0592 (Re<sub>x</sub>\*)<sup>−0.2</sup> with C<sub>H</sub>\* = ½ c<sub>f</sub>\* Pr\*<sup>−2/3</sup>. A Lees–Mangler running length carries the flat-plate results onto the axisymmetric body:

<Equation tex={String.raw`x_\text{eff} = \frac{\int_0^{s}\rho_e\mu_e u_e r^2\,ds'}{\rho_e\mu_e u_e r^2}`} />

On a sharp cone this gives x<sub>eff</sub> = s/3, the √3 Mangler factor, which is one of the required test limits. The stagnation point uses Fay–Riddell without dissociation:

<Equation tex={String.raw`q_s = 0.76\,Pr_w^{-0.6}(\rho_e\mu_e)^{0.4}(\rho_w\mu_w)^{0.1}\sqrt{(du_e/dx)_s}\,(h_{0e} - h_w)`} />

Fin leading edges are swept cylinders. Transition is a fully laminar or fully turbulent bound or, for nominal runs, a criterion (Quinn–Gong by default) with an intermittency blend. Ablator gas injection reduces heating by C<sub>H</sub>/C<sub>H0</sub> = φ/(e<sup>φ</sup> − 1), φ proportional to the injected mass flux.

### In-depth charring response

In surface-fixed coordinates, with recession rate ṡ and pyrolysis-gas mass flux G flowing toward the surface, the energy equation is the CMA form:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\rho c_p\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(Ak\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right) + (h_g - \bar h)\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + G\,\frac{\partial h_g}{\partial y} + \dot s\,\rho c_p\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}`} />

A(y) carries the wall curvature. The resin decomposes by multi-component Arrhenius kinetics (TACOT data for the benchmark material):

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{\partial\rho_i}{\partial t} = -B_i\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_i}{\mathcal R T}\right)\rho_{v,i}\left(\frac{\rho_i-\rho_{c,i}}{\rho_{v,i}}\right)^{\psi_i}`} />

Sources, cited by title: Anderson, *Hypersonic and High-Temperature Gas Dynamics*; Tauber, "A Review of High-Speed, Convective, Heat-Transfer Computation Methods" (NASA TP-2914); Quinn and Gong, NASA TP-2000-209034; Fay and Riddell, "Theory of Stagnation Point Heat Transfer in Dissociated Air"; Moyer and Rindal, NASA CR-1061 (the CMA in-depth formulation); and the Ablation Workshop test cases. Every equation in the code maps to a physics-spec ID. In the first pass all 82 items were checked against page images of their sources: 57 verified (11 of them after correction), 11 partly verified and 14 settled as recorded modelling decisions.

### Numerics

- **Grid.** Vertex-centred finite differences in conservative form, with a node on every layer interface and harmonic face conductances, so energy is conserved across jumps in k. Spacing grows geometrically from 20 µm at the surface (ratio ≤ 1.15, ≤ 80 nodes).
- **Time stepping.** Backward Euler by default, L-stable for the stiff heating pulse and the T<sup>4</sup> radiation term; Crank–Nicolson for convergence studies. Adaptive Δt: accept if max ΔT ≤ 5 K, reaction-extent change ≤ 0.02 and Newton converges in ≤ 8 iterations, else halve.
- **Coupling.** Newton–Raphson with an analytic Jacobian, the convective flux implicit, and an exact separable pyrolysis update inside each iterate. All stations form one sparse block-tridiagonal system; the ablator grid contracts with recession and remaps conservatively.
- **Audits.** Discrete energy and mass audits close on every run, and no wall temperature on ascent may exceed the stagnation temperature.

### Assumptions and scope

Axisymmetric flow at zero angle of attack; calorically perfect inviscid relations; 1-D conduction per station; quasi-steady pyrolysis gas in local thermal equilibrium. Out of scope for version 1: chemistry, shock-layer radiation, angle of attack, shock and fin–body interactions, tumbling descent and thermal stress.

**Caveats on this run.** The nose wall in this example is 8.3 mm thick, more than the local nose radius over roughly the first 12 mm of the nose. A 1-D through-the-wall model cannot represent that region, so its 17 stations are excluded from conduction, as are the six fin leading-edge stations, whose wall model is still pending. Those stations get only a heating-only surface estimate, a radiative-equilibrium temperature, which peaks at 1229.9 K at the nose tip at 16.9 s. They have no in-depth or bond-line result, and the simulator refuses sizing and bond-line results for any component with an excluded station, rather than guessing. With the placeholder phenolic, the virgin front ends at most 0.12 mm below the surface and nothing recedes. The bond-line check is worst on a fin face, where the peak temperature (666.9 K) is 256.9 K above the placeholder 410 K limit, and the tool flags it. With placeholder properties this demonstrates the check; it is not a finding about a real heat shield.

<Callout title="Three kinds of check">
**Verification** asks whether the code solves its own equations (exact and manufactured solutions). **Code-to-code** compares against another code. **Validation** compares against measurements. Acceptance criteria for every case were written before the comparison was run, and nothing is tuned to the data.

### Verification: conduction and recession

The conduction suite (34 tests, 304 gated checks, none failed) uses exact solutions (semi-infinite solid, convecting slab, steady composite wall) and manufactured solutions on planar, cylindrical and spherical walls. Observed orders: 2.0005–2.0006 in space; 0.993–0.994 in time for backward Euler, 1.971–1.976 for Crank–Nicolson. The worst energy-audit residual is 1.1e−13. A manufactured solution on a receding surface (method of "Numerical Modeling of Ablation Heat Transfer", Ewing, Laker and Walker) gives the expected first order, p = 1.001–1.012. At the last milestone gate, 55/55 verification tests and all 21 tests in the validation suite (flight-data and code-to-code cases) passed.

### Code-to-code: Ablation Workshop Test Case #1

The hero figure on the Overview tab. All 55 temperature points are within max(3 %, 25 K) of FIAT; the worst is +17.4 K at 1 mm and 1 s (61 % of its tolerance). The RMS difference is 6.6 K, or 0.864 % in the relative-error form of a published PATO–FIAT comparison. Both pyrolysis fronts are within 0.5 mm at all 22 points, and gas flux is within 10 % from 5 s on (worst +1.14 %). I used TACOT 3.0 where the test case cites version 1.5, a known input difference. Test Cases 2.1 and 2.2 (surface chemistry, recession) run but stay report-only until reference curves are available.

### Limitations

- **Transition location** is the largest uncertainty (a factor of 3–5 in local heating), so results are a laminar / nominal / turbulent envelope and sizing assumes fully turbulent flow.
- **Validation Mach range.** The flight case reaches Mach 3.6. Higher-Mach flight cases, such as the HIFiRE-1 ascent (Mach 3 to 5.8), are in the validation plan but not yet run.
- **Material data.** Verified ablator data from the lab are still being collected. Placeholder properties are guarded and watermarked, so they cannot reach a design result.
- **Below about Mach 3** the Newtonian stagnation velocity gradient is inaccurate (±30 %), though heating there is small.
- **1-D and axisymmetric:** no lateral conduction and no angle of attack.

### Toward graduate work

The simulator rests on engineering correlations. Its largest uncertainties (transition, entropy-layer effects on blunted noses, fin–body interference) are where those correlations are weakest and where boundary-layer and CFD analysis can do better. Those are the questions I want to work on next. The [Research Interests](/research/) page says more.

### Methods note

The specifications, MATLAB code and tests were written with AI coding assistants (Claude Code and Codex) under my direction. The process is built to catch their mistakes: equations checked against source page images, tests written blind to the implementation, independent physics and code reviews on every task, and a gate at each milestone where I review the evidence and accept or reject the modelling decisions. No tolerance is loosened without a recorded decision.
