<TabPanel id="overview">

A tsunami slows down as the water gets shallower, because the long-wave speed scales as √h. This project solves the linearised shallow-water wave equation over a basin whose depth varies, and shows the wavefront bending as it passes a shoal.

<Video src="/media/tsunami.mp4" poster={poster} alt="Animation of a single wave pulse launched from the left edge, crossing a long basin over a variable seabed, bending over a shoal and reflecting from the far wall." source="re-run of projects/tsunami-simulation (same scheme, vectorised) in .scratch/">
The wave-maker on the left launches one pulse. The front refracts over the shoal near x = 2 and reflects from the walls. I re-ran my scheme in vectorised form to render this video; the stencil, boundaries and depth function are unchanged.
</Video>

</TabPanel>

<TabPanel id="technical">

<Equation tex={String.raw`u_{tt} = \nabla\cdot\big(h(x,y)\,\nabla u\big) = h\,\nabla^2 u + h_x u_x + h_y u_y`} />

Second-order central differences in space and time give an explicit update in which each neighbour carries its own weight. For example, the right neighbour has weight λ(h + Δx h<sub>x</sub>/2), with λ = (cΔt/Δx)². The boundary conditions are:

- **Left:** a moving wave-maker, u = 0.3 sin(2πt) for t < 0.5 (one half-sine pulse), then held at zero.
- **Right:** zero slope, ∂u/∂x = 0, through a phantom node.
- **Top and bottom:** reflecting walls through mirrored phantom nodes.

The depth field has a beach-like slope and a shoal, h = 1 − 0.04xy + 0.4((x−2)/3)² + 0.4(y−0.75)². With h<sub>max</sub> ≈ 1.86, the Courant number √h<sub>max</sub>·cΔt/Δx = 0.56, which is inside the 1/√2 limit for this 2-D explicit scheme.

</TabPanel>

<TabPanel id="reflection">

The hard Dirichlet cut-off of the wave-maker at t = 0.5 adds high-frequency content, which shows up as grid-scale ripples behind the pulse. A smoother pulse (or a wave-maker with a ramp) would remove it. The linear model has no wave steepening, breaking or run-up. Those need the nonlinear shallow-water equations, a conservative finite-volume scheme and wetting/drying, which is the natural next step toward a real inundation model.

</TabPanel>
